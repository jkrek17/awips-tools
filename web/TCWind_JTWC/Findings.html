Findings: how well does the tool actually work?

A plain summary of how well this tool's storm-size and wind-field guesses match real JTWC data. This is a fixed summary, not a live view - to explore a real storm yourself, see Archived Cases in the live tool.

1Where this data comes from

  • Live tool ("Fetch bulletins" / "Paste text"): the same real-time JTWC warning bulletins this tool's GFE version consumes operationally - forecast strength, position, and whatever quadrant wind radii the bulletin happens to carry at issuance time.
  • Archived Cases and everything on this page: JTWC's own post-season best-track reanalysis for each storm - the corrected, final position, intensity (Vmax), quadrant wind radii (R34/R50/R64), RMW (radius of maximum wind), and ROCI (radius of outermost closed isobar), pulled from NOAA's public IBTrACS archive, agency jtwc_wp.
  • Scope: 2005-2024, Western Pacific basin, IBTrACS-flagged tropical-storm-strength (Vmax ≥ 34 kt) and stronger only. JTWC's R50/R64 reporting for this basin was inconsistent before 2005 - see "Why this page starts at 2005" below for the numbers behind that cutoff. Depression-strength records are excluded because the tool itself now skips them (see below); 2025-2026 storms aren't finalized in IBTrACS yet.
  • In short: the live tool's output is a real-time guess, made before the storm's true structure is known. Everything below scores that same guess logic against the after-the-fact answer, field by field, on a dataset the tool's own fitting never saw in its held-out portion.

2What we found

Storm size · fixed

The Rmax regression is refit, but the radii clamp gives some bias back

  • Willoughby (2006)'s original Atlantic-fit coefficients, scored against 2005-2024 WestPac RMW (n=8906): bias −6.1 nm, MAE 10.1 nm, RMSE 16.0 nm, r=0.56.
  • Refit on WestPac best-track data, scored on a held-out 20% never used for fitting: bias −1.9 nm, MAE 8.7 nm, RMSE 14.1 nm, r=0.59 - most of the systematic undersizing is gone.
  • But that's the regression alone. resolveRmax() as actually run - regression, then clamped to ≤0.7× the smallest reported radius at the highest threshold present - comes back to bias −5.6 nm (MAE 9.2, RMSE 15.0, r=0.59). The clamp binds on 40% of records (n=3535), and when it does it's the dominant source of the tool's remaining negative bias - see "hard ceiling" below.
  • By category: TS (34-63 kt) MAE 12.1 nm, r=0.35; TY+ (64 kt+) MAE 5.6 nm, r=0.43. Skill is meaningfully better at typhoon strength, where more storms carry enough reported radii to constrain the clamp.
Storm size · scope

The tool skips depressions entirely

  • Below tropical-storm strength (Vmax < 34 kt) there is no organized wind field to model in JTWC's own convention, and almost never any reported wind radii to constrain one from even if there were.
  • buildFor() now leaves the background wind grid untouched for a depression-strength storm-time rather than fabricate structure the record doesn't support.
  • Every check on this page is therefore scored only against TS-strength-or-greater records, to match what the tool actually attempts.
Storm size (Rmax) · structural limit

The regression has a hard ceiling near 39 nm

  • Across all 8906 in-scope records, resolveRmax() never returns more than 38.7 nm - even before the radii clamp applies. Actual RMW in the same dataset ranges up to 155 nm, and 21.9% of records with a reported RMW exceed this ceiling.
  • This is not primarily a radii-clamp artifact - the ceiling shows up in the unclamped regression itself. Because willoughbyRmax() is exponential and decreasing in Vmax, and the tool only ever runs it for Vmax ≥ 34 kt, the largest value the fitted curve can return over the Vmax/latitude combinations this basin actually produces lands right at ~39 nm (the weakest in-scope intensity, at the archive's higher WestPac latitudes). No further-north, weaker-storm case exists in-scope that could push it higher - it's a property of the fitted curve, not a downstream clamp.
  • Net effect: a genuinely large but weak system - the case this matters most for - will be undersized by the model regardless of whether any radii are reported at all.
  • Wind-field extent (checked against ROCI, at the radius where modeled wind falls below 10/15/20 kt): tropical storms run about 22-75 nm too small depending on threshold; typhoons track closer, except at the 10 kt threshold, where the field overshoots ROCI by about 65 nm on average.
Wind field shape

Least certain just outside the eyewall

  • Cross-checked the tool's profile against an independently fit Holland (1980) profile, both anchored to the same Vmax, Rmax, and reported R34. (Holland's own B parameter, fit only to R34, predicts the reported R50/R64 reasonably well on its own - r=0.85/0.70, MAE 12.0/12.9 nm - which is why it's a defensible independent baseline here.)
  • The two profiles disagree most in the Rmax-R64 zone - mean |difference| 11.8 kt, p90 up to 68.1 kt - and converge steadily moving outward (R50-R34: 3.2 kt mean; beyond R34: 4.6 kt mean).
  • That inner zone, just outside the eyewall, is also where a wind-speed error matters most operationally.
Storm size (Rmax) · caveat

Storm type predicts Rmax error far better than distance to land

  • Same Rmax-vs-RMW check as above, split by IBTrACS' own storm-nature flag: purely tropical (TS, n=8598) MAE 8.6 nm, r=0.60. Mixed structure (MX, n=270) MAE 24.9 nm, r=0.20; extratropical transition (ET, n=56) MAE 20.1 nm, r=0.16 - roughly triple the error, with almost no skill left tracking any individual storm's size.
  • Split instead by distance to the nearest coastline, error is nearly flat: MAE 8.2 nm over/at the coast (≤25 km) versus 9.5 nm in the open ocean (>300 km). Rmax skill does not meaningfully degrade approaching land - which matters, since that's exactly where watches and warnings concentrate.
  • Same failure signature (fast-moving, transitioning, structurally asymmetric storms) as the motion-asymmetry double-count bug fixed earlier in this project. The vortex model assumes a steady tropical circulation; transitioning storms often aren't one, wherever they happen to be relative to land.
Data coverage · scope

Why this page starts at 2005, not 2001

  • JTWC's WestPac best-track record carries RMW and some wind radii back to 2001, but reporting of the finer R50/R64 thresholds specifically was inconsistent before 2005: only about 26% of typhoon-strength (64 kt+) 2000-2004 records carried an R64 radius at all, versus 86-94% from 2005 onward.
  • Every figure on this page, the refit regression itself, and the Archived Cases storm browser now draw exclusively from 2005-2024 - the fully radii-capable window - so "the archive" means the same dataset everywhere on this site.
  • Practical effect: a pre-2005 storm won't appear in Archived Cases at all now. A storm that shows only a 34 kt ring there is a genuine JTWC reporting gap within the 2005-2024 window, not a display bug and not a pre-2005 artifact.

3The numbers

Storm core size (Rmax) - NOT the 34/50/64 kt wind radii used elsewhere on this page

Rmax is how far out from the center the strongest winds are found - a tight radius right around the eye, always much smaller than the wind radii. This compares the tool's Rmax guess to JTWC's real, corrected value (called RMW in the record), for every usable Western Pacific tropical storm or typhoon from 2005-2024 (depressions are out of scope - see "The tool skips depressions entirely" above). The diagonal line is a perfect match; dots below it mean the tool guessed too small - notice how the tool's own guesses (across the bottom) barely spread past ~39 nm at all, even as the real size (up the left) ranges past 150 nm - the structural ceiling described above. Colored by storm strength: purple = typhoon, orange = tropical storm.
