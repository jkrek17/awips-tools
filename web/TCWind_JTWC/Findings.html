About this page

This tool builds a tropical cyclone wind field from a JTWC (Joint Typhoon Warning Center) warning’s position, intensity, motion and quadrant wind radii, using NHC’s (National Hurricane Center’s) own Gridded Tropical Cyclone Model (GTCM), the same construction NHC’s Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch uses to build the real Atlantic and East Pacific gridded TCM products from Best Track and advisory data. The question this page answers is simple: does this tool actually produce a GTCM-style field from the bulletin text alone? Short answer: yes. Fit to the 34 and 50 kt radii alone and asked to predict the 64 kt radius it was never shown, the modelled field correlates with the real reported radius at —, the signature of an actual least-squares fit, not a lookup table.

Nothing here is checked against an observed wind; see the EXPERIMENTAL banner above and Also worth knowing below for the caveats that matter most.

What GTCM is designed to do

GTCM is not a numerical forecast model. It is one symmetric vortex plus one wavenumber-1 term for the asymmetry a storm’s own motion adds — stronger wind ahead and to the right of track, weaker behind and to the left. That is the whole shape.

The vortex is fitted by weighted least squares, chosen to minimize the error in wind speed against the reported radii. It is not built to pass through every one of them exactly, and it cannot: a single smooth vortex has far fewer free parameters than the twelve independent radii (three thresholds × four quadrants) a bulletin can report, so something has to give.

That is deliberate, not a shortcoming. NHC’s own Gridded TCM Users Guide specifies the fit this way on purpose: forcing one vortex through every reported radius exactly produces a field with a corner at every quadrant boundary, because interpolating between independently reported numbers puts a slope break there by construction. NHC’s Tropical Analysis and Forecast Branch accepts the small, systematic gap this leaves against the reported radii (read next) in exchange for a field with one coherent gradient belonging to the model, not to wherever the compass bisectors happen to fall — and this tool implements that same trade.

Quadrant maximum vs quadrant average

This is the single most misreadable number about this tool. Read on its own, a modelled isotach landing inside the reported radius looks like a systematic undersize of roughly a sixth, and the tempting conclusion would be that the tool is broken. It is not: the two numbers are measuring different things.

What the bulletin reports is the maximum extent of that wind speed anywhere in the quadrant: the farthest point at which, say, 34 kt is analysed in the northeast 90°.

What the GTCM fit aims at is the quadrant average radius, because the model is one smooth vortex and the guide specifies the fit that way (Users Guide step 2c). The reported radius is multiplied by — to get the fit target before the least squares is run. That factor is read from the shipped code when this page loads, not typed into it, so it cannot drift from the model it describes.

So a modelled isotach that lands about a sixth inside the reported radius is the model doing exactly what it was told. Neither number is the “real” one on its own; both are true at once, and read together, not instead of each other.
